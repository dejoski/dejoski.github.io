The Triumphant Messiah

Acts 4:23–31
1Why do the nations rage
and the peoples plot in vain?
2The kings of the earth take their stand
and the rulers gather together,
against the Lord
and against His Anointed One:
3“Let us break Their chains
and cast away Their cords.”
4The One enthroned in heaven laughs;
the Lord taunts them.
5Then He rebukes them in His anger,
and terrifies them in His fury:
6“I have installed My King on Zion,
upon My holy mountain.”
7I will proclaim the decree
spoken to Me by the Lord:
“You are My Son;
today I have become Your Father.
8Ask Me, and I will make the nations Your inheritance,
the ends of the earth Your possession.
9You will break them with an iron scepter;
You will shatter them like pottery.”
10Therefore be wise, O kings;
be admonished, O judges of the earth.
11Serve the Lord with fear,
and rejoice with trembling.
12Kiss the Son, lest He be angry
and you perish in your rebellion,
when His wrath ignites in an instant.
Blessed are all who take refuge in Him.

Study notes on this chapter · 3

Prophecy & fulfillmentLinked by the New Testament

Why do the nations rage?

After Peter and John are threatened by the council, the believers pray with Psalm 2's opening lines about kings and rulers gathering against the LORD and his anointed. They read Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel as the ones who gathered against Jesus.

When: A royal psalm from the monarchy; the believers' prayer in Jerusalem, c. AD 30–35.

Original passagePsalms 2:1–2
Where the New Testament applies itActs 4:23–28

Prophecy & fulfillmentLinked by the New Testament

You are my Son

A royal psalm in which God declares the anointed king to be his son. The New Testament applies these words to Jesus in Paul's preaching about the resurrection and in Hebrews (the voice at Jesus' baptism also echoes them).

When: A royal psalm from the era of Israel's monarchy; applied in Acts and Hebrews, c. AD 30–65.

Original passagePsalms 2:7
Where the New Testament applies itActs 13:33Hebrews 1:5Hebrews 5:5

Prophecy & fulfillmentDebated

The woman and the dragon

John sees a woman clothed with the sun who gives birth to a male child destined to rule the nations, while a red dragon waits to devour him. The child is caught up to God and the woman flees to the wilderness. The passage draws on Genesis 3, Joseph's dream and Psalm 2.

When: Revelation, c. AD 90s.

Original passageRevelation 12:1–6Revelation 12:13–17
Echoed inGenesis 3:15Genesis 37:9–10Psalms 2:9
Ways people read this (4)
  • The woman is Israel (or the faithful remnant), from whom the Messiah came.
  • The woman is the church, persecuted by Satan, and the child represents Christ or the church's faithful.
  • The woman is Mary, the mother of Jesus (a common Catholic and Orthodox reading alongside others), with the larger picture being the church.
  • The woman is a figure for the whole people of God, both Israel and the church, from Eve to the end.
